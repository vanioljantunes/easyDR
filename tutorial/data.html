The table

Long form, one row per exposure category per study. easyDR does not ask you to reshape, sort or aggregate it, and it guesses the column names when you leave them out. Two bundled tables show the shape to copy.

ColumnWhat it holds
studlabThe study label. Rows sharing one are one study.
doseThe numeric dose you assigned to that category. Never inferred from a category range.
refOptional. Marks each study's reference category. When absent one is inferred and the guesses are printed.
designOptional. Case-control, incidence rate, or cumulative incidence. Each study keeps its own.
You assign the dose yourself.

A published table reports a category, say 10 to 20 g/day. Turning that into one number means choosing the midpoint, the median intake, or whatever the authors reported, and the choice changes the slope. easyDR will not make it for you. Chapter 2 of the book is mostly about making it defensibly.

sm decides which columns are read

smOutcomeColumns read
"RR"risk ratioevent.e, n.e   or   TE, lower, upper
"OR"odds ratio, from a case-control designevent.e, n.e   or   TE, lower, upper
"IRR"incidence rate ratioevent.e, with person-time in n.e
"MD"mean differencemean.e, sd.e, n.e
"SMD"standardised mean differencemean.e, sd.e, n.e

Pass a column belonging to the other kind of outcome and easyDR stops. Ignoring the name would leave you with a fit that looks as though it used your standard deviations when it did not. 

easydr(alcohol_cancer, sm = "IRR", shape = "linear",
       sd.e = "sd")
#> Error: easydr: sm = "IRR" is a binary outcome,
#>   so sd.e is not read. Drop it or change sm.

When the table has no counts

Greenland-Longnecker and Hamling both need denominators. A table carrying only an effect with its limits supports neither, so easyDR treats the contrasts as independent and prints that it did. This understates the correlation. Supply the counts whenever the paper gives them.

Chapter 2 of the book has the extraction sheet that produces a table in this shape.
