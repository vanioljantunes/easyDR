The shaded rows are each study's reference category, conventionally its lowest dose.

The columns

studyStudy label. Rows sharing a label are one study. doseThe exposure for that category, in a unit shared across studies. casesEvents in the category. nThe denominator: group size, or person-time for rate data.
Doses must already be comparable. The check looks at structure, not units. If one study reports grams per day and another cups per week, the frame passes every check and the pooled curve is meaningless. Converting is extraction work, and chapter 2 covers it.

Before the model: dr_viability()

Two-stage methods need at least three exposure levels per study. A study with two points carries no information about curvature, and its within-study covariance cannot be reconstructed (Greenland and Longnecker, 1992). That is the floor, and three is the default.

A dataset that passes

dr_viability(good)
Dose-response viability study levels has_reference two_stage_ok 1 Allen 2011 4 TRUE TRUE 2 Brown 2014 4 TRUE TRUE 3 Chen 2019 4 TRUE TRUE No blocking problems found.

A dataset that does not

dr_viability(bad)
study levels has_reference two_stage_ok 1 Allen 2011 3 TRUE TRUE 2 Brown 2014 2 TRUE FALSE 3 Chen 2019 3 TRUE TRUE Problems: - 1 study(ies) with fewer than 3 exposure levels (Brown 2014): a two-stage fit drops these. Consider one-stage. - missing values in 'cases': the within-study covariance cannot be reconstructed.

A failure is not the end

One-stage methods keep studies with two exposure levels, and that is precisely their selling point (Crippa et al., 2019). Lowering the floor shows what a one-stage model would have to work with: 

dr_viability(bad, min_levels = 2)

The missing count is still reported, because no choice of model recovers it.
